Collision theory

For a reaction to happen, particles must collide with each other with enough energy. This minimum energy is called the activation energy. Anything that increases how often particles collide, or how much energy those collisions have, speeds up the reaction.

The four factors that change rate of reaction

Temperature ↑ → particles move faster (more kinetic energy) → more frequent collisions, and more of them have enough energy to react → faster rate.

Concentration ↑ (of a dissolved reactant) → more particles in the same volume → more frequent collisions → faster rate.

Surface area ↑ (smaller pieces of a solid) → more particles exposed and available to react → more frequent collisions → faster rate.

Catalyst → provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy, without being used up itself → faster rate.

Examiner tip: Always finish the explanation with "…so more frequent collisions" or "…so more frequent successful collisions." A lot of marks are lost because students stop at "particles move faster" without linking it back to collisions.

Measuring rate of reaction

You can measure how fast a reaction happens by tracking, at regular time intervals:

• Volume of gas produced (using a gas syringe), good for reactions that make a gas, e.g. acid + metal, acid + carbonate.

• Change in mass, since the mass of the flask decreases as gas escapes.

• Time for a solution to turn cloudy/opaque, used for reactions that produce a precipitate, e.g. sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid.

Mean rate of reaction = amount of product formed (or reactant used up) ÷ time taken.

Don't write this: "The mass of the flask increased" for the thiosulfate/acid reaction. It decreases, because a gas (sulfur dioxide) is made and escapes into the air. The atoms aren't lost, they've just left the container.

Reversible reactions & equilibrium

In a reversible reaction, the products can react again to re-form the original reactants, shown with a ⇌ symbol. If a reversible reaction happens in a closed system (nothing can escape or be added), it eventually reaches equilibrium. The forward and backward reactions are still happening, but at the same rate, so the amounts of reactants and products stop changing.

Changing the conditions (temperature, pressure, or concentration) can shift the position of equilibrium, favouring either the forward or backward reaction. At this stage, you mainly need to recognise that the reaction can go both ways, and that equilibrium means "no further overall change."

Le Chatelier's principle: how conditions shift equilibrium

If a system at equilibrium is disturbed, the position of equilibrium shifts to counteract the change:

• Temperature ↑ shifts equilibrium in the endothermic direction (to absorb the extra energy). Temperature ↓ shifts it in the exothermic direction.

• Pressure ↑ (gas reactions) shifts equilibrium towards the side with fewer gas molecules.

• A catalyst does not change the position of equilibrium. It speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached faster but the amounts of reactants/products at equilibrium stay the same.

• Removing a product shifts equilibrium towards that side, to replace what's been removed.

Worked example (Haber process): N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), forward reaction exothermic. Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium left (endothermic direction), so the yield of ammonia decreases. Removing ammonia shifts equilibrium right, so more ammonia is made to replace what's lost.

5. A gas syringe is used to collect the gas from a reaction, and its measurements are taken every 30 seconds until the reaction stops. What type of variable is time in this investigation? (1 mark)

Watch out: Here you choose when to take each reading (every 30 seconds), so time is the independent variable and goes on the x axis. The volume of gas is what you measure, so it is the dependent variable on the y axis. Time is only the dependent variable when it is the thing you measure, such as the time for the cross to disappear.

6. Describe how you could investigate the effect of acid concentration on the rate of reaction between hydrochloric acid and magnesium, and how you would make it a fair test. You do not need to write about safety precautions. (4–6 marks)


Model answer:
  • Measure a fixed volume of hydrochloric acid using a measuring cylinder and add it to a conical flask.
  • Add a weighed piece of magnesium and quickly attach a bung fitted to a gas syringe.
  • Start a stopwatch immediately.
  • Measure the volume of gas collected at regular time intervals (e.g. every 30 seconds) until the reaction finishes.
  • Repeat with different concentrations of acid.
To make it a fair test: use the same volume of acid, the same starting temperature, and the same mass/length of magnesium each time. Only the concentration of acid should change.

  1. Use a beaker to measure 50 cm³ of heated sodium thiosulfate solution into a conical flask.
  2. Measure the temperature of the room.
  3. Put the conical flask on a black cross drawn on a piece of paper.
  4. Start a timer.
  5. Use the same beaker to measure 10 cm³ of hydrochloric acid into the conical flask.
  6. Stop the timer when the cross is no longer visible.

Describe a method the students should use to produce accurate results. You do not need to write about safety precautions. (6 marks)


Go through the method line by line. What's wrong with using a beaker, measuring the room instead of the solution, starting the timer too early, and re-using the same beaker?
Model answer:
  • Use a measuring cylinder (not a beaker) to measure 50 cm³ of sodium thiosulfate.
  • Use a different measuring cylinder to measure 10 cm³ of hydrochloric acid.

11. Calculating the mean rate from a graph

A graph shows loss of mass (g) against time (mins) for a reaction, with a curved decreasing line. Calculate the mean rate of reaction between 1 minute and 3 minutes, given that the loss of mass was 0.19 g at 1 minute and 0.34 g at 3 minutes. (2 marks)


Change in mass = 0.34 − 0.19 = 0.15 g. Change in time = 3 − 1 = 2 minutes.
Mean rate of reaction = 0.15 ÷ 2 = 0.075 g/min
Method: a mean rate between two times is the change in the quantity ÷ the change in time, read straight off the curve. You only draw a tangent when a question asks for the rate at one particular time: draw it at that time, then its gradient (change in y ÷ change in x) is the rate.

12. Haber process equilibrium (Le Chatelier)

Ammonia is produced by the reaction: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g). The forward reaction is exothermic.

(a) Explain the effect of increasing temperature on the yield of ammonia at equilibrium. (2 marks)


The equilibrium shifts to the left, in the endothermic direction. The yield of ammonia will decrease.

(b) Ammonia is removed from the reaction mixture as it forms. Explain the effect on the position of equilibrium. (2 marks)


The equilibrium shifts to the right, so that more ammonia will be made to replace what is lost.

(c) What is the effect of a catalyst on the equilibrium position? (2 marks)


The equilibrium position stays the same. A catalyst increases the rate of the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount, so equilibrium is reached faster but the yield is unchanged.

Exothermic and endothermic reactions

An exothermic reaction releases energy to the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings increases. Examples: combustion, neutralisation, most oxidation reactions.

An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings, so the temperature of the surroundings decreases. Examples: thermal decomposition, dissolving some salts.

Don't write this: "heat is given off". Examiners prefer "energy is released to the surroundings." If you use the word "transferred," you must say transferred to the surroundings, since on its own "transferred" isn't specific enough. Stick to the word energy rather than "heat."

Reaction profiles

A reaction profile is a diagram showing how energy changes during a reaction. It shows:

• The energy level of the reactants and of the products

• The activation energy: the "hump" that must be overcome for the reaction to start

• The overall energy change: the difference between reactants' and products' energy levels

In an exothermic reaction profile, the products end up at a lower energy than the reactants (energy has been released). In an endothermic reaction profile, the products end up at a higher energy than the reactants (energy has been absorbed).

This example shows an exothermic reaction profile. Notice the products end lower than the reactants.

Calculating energy change using bond energies

Every chemical bond stores a certain amount of energy (its bond energy, in kJ/mol). During a reaction:

• Breaking bonds in the reactants requires energy (endothermic): total = sum of bond energies broken.

• Forming new bonds in the products releases energy (exothermic): total = sum of bond energies formed.

Overall energy change = energy needed to break bonds − energy released forming bonds. A negative answer means the reaction is exothermic overall; a positive answer means it's endothermic overall.

Worked example: Combustion of methane, CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Bond energies: C–H = 413, O=O = 498, C=O = 805, O–H = 464 kJ/mol.
Bonds broken: 4×(C–H) + 2×(O=O) = 4×413 + 2×498 = 1652 + 996 = 2648 kJ/mol.
Bonds formed: 2×(C=O) + 4×(O–H) = 2×805 + 4×464 = 1610 + 1856 = 3466 kJ/mol.
Overall energy change = 2648 − 3466 = −818 kJ/mol (exothermic, as expected for combustion).

Required practical: Temperature changes (neutralisation)

This required practical investigates the energy change when an acid is neutralised by an alkali.

Method outline: Measure a fixed volume of dilute hydrochloric acid into an insulated cup (e.g. a polystyrene cup inside a beaker, for stability and to reduce heat loss). Record the starting temperature. Add small, measured volumes of sodium hydroxide solution one at a time, stirring gently and recording the highest temperature reached after each addition. Repeat the whole experiment to get a mean temperature for each volume added.

Analysing the results: Plot temperature against volume of alkali added. The temperature rises as neutralisation releases energy (exothermic), reaches a maximum at the point of exact neutralisation, then falls as further alkali added simply dilutes and cools the mixture.

Examiner tip: An insulated cup and a lid with a hole for the thermometer both reduce energy loss to the surroundings. Mentioning *why* a control step matters, not just naming it, picks up extra marks.

Recap: ionic bonding (from Year 9)

Ionic bonds form between a metal and a non-metal. Electrons are transferred from the metal atom to the non-metal atom, forming oppositely charged ions that are strongly attracted to each other. Millions of ions arrange into a giant ionic lattice.

Covalent bonding (new for Year 10)

Covalent bonds form between two non-metals. Instead of transferring electrons, the atoms share a pair of electrons, one from each atom, so both atoms get a fuller outer shell. Covalent bonds are very strong.

Simple examples: H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CH₄, and O₂ (a double covalent bond).

Dot-and-cross diagram of a hydrogen molecule (H₂): one shared pair of electrons, one from each atom.

Properties linked to structure

Ionic compounds (giant lattice): high melting and boiling points (strong electrostatic forces of attraction act throughout the whole giant lattice, and lots of energy is needed to overcome them). Conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water (ions are free to move and carry charge), but not when solid (ions are fixed in place).

Simple covalent molecules (e.g. H₂O, CO₂): low melting and boiling points, and don't conduct electricity (no overall electric charge, and no free electrons or ions).

Don't write this: "Simple covalent substances have low melting points because the covalent bonds are weak." This is one of the most common mark-scheme traps. The covalent bonds themselves are strong. It's the weak forces of attraction between different molecules (intermolecular forces) that need only a little energy to overcome. Always specify "the forces between molecules," not "the covalent bonds."

Giant covalent structures

Diamond: each carbon atom forms four strong covalent bonds in a rigid 3D lattice. Very hard, very high melting point, doesn't conduct electricity (no free electrons or ions).

Graphite: each carbon atom forms three covalent bonds, arranged in layers. High melting point. The layers have no covalent bonds between them, so they can slide over each other, and graphite is soft and slippery. Each carbon has one spare (delocalised) electron, which is free to move, so unlike diamond, graphite conducts electricity.

Graphene and fullerenes

Graphene is a single layer of graphite, one atom thick: a sheet of carbon atoms each bonded to three others in hexagons. It's strong, light, and conducts electricity (delocalised electrons), making it useful in electronics and composite materials.

Fullerenes are molecules of carbon atoms arranged as hollow shapes: tubes, spheres (like buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀), or other cages. Fullerenes can be used to deliver drugs into the body, as lubricants, and as catalysts. Carbon nanotubes are extremely strong and used to strengthen materials, and can conduct electricity due to delocalised electrons.

Metallic bonding

A metal is a giant structure of atoms arranged in regular layers. Each metal atom loses the electrons in its outermost shell, forming positive metal ions. These electrons don't stay attached to any one atom. They become delocalised, forming a "sea" of electrons that can move freely throughout the whole structure.

Metallic bonding is the strong electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the negative sea of delocalised electrons.

Green circles = positive metal ions in fixed layers. Red dots = delocalised electrons, free to move anywhere.

Properties explained by metallic bonding

High melting/boiling points: strong metallic bonds throughout the giant structure need lots of energy to overcome.

Good conductors of electricity and heat: the delocalised electrons are free to move and carry charge/energy through the structure.

Malleable (can be bent/hammered into shape): the layers of ions can slide over each other without breaking the metallic bonds, since the sea of electrons moves with them and keeps holding the structure together.

Alloys

An alloy is a mixture of a metal with one or more other elements (usually another metal, or carbon). Pure metals are often too soft for practical use, so alloys are made to improve their properties. For example, steel (iron + carbon) is harder than pure iron.

Alloys are harder than pure metals because the different-sized atoms distort the regular layers, making it harder for the layers to slide over each other.

Don't write this: "Alloys are harder because the atoms are bigger" on its own. You must explain why that matters: different-sized atoms distort the regular arrangement of layers, so the layers can't slide over each other as easily as in a pure metal.

The reactivity series decides how a metal is extracted

An ore is a rock that contains enough of a metal (usually as a metal compound) to make it worth extracting. The reactivity series tells you exactly which extraction method to use, because it compares each metal's reactivity to carbon:

Three extraction routes

More reactive than carbon (potassium → aluminium): extracted using electrolysis. This uses large amounts of energy and is expensive, but it's the only method powerful enough to separate very reactive metals from their compounds. You'll learn how electrolysis works in Year 11.

Less reactive than carbon (zinc → copper): extracted by reduction with carbon. Carbon is more reactive than these metals, so it can displace them from their oxides. Carbon takes the oxygen, and the pure metal is left behind.

Very unreactive (silver, gold): found in the Earth as the pure, uncombined metal, called the native state, so they don't need to be chemically extracted at all.

Oxidation and reduction

Reduction = loss of oxygen. Oxidation = gain of oxygen. When carbon reduces a metal oxide, the metal oxide is reduced (loses oxygen) and the carbon is oxidised (gains oxygen, forming carbon dioxide).

Two alternative methods are used instead:

Both routes lead to a leachate. The pure metal is then obtained by displacement or electrolysis.

Getting pure metal from the leachate

Displacement: adding a more reactive metal (e.g. scrap iron) to the leachate causes it to displace the less reactive target metal (e.g. copper) from solution. Iron goes into solution, and pure copper is deposited.

Electrolysis can also be used to obtain the pure metal from the leachate. You'll learn how this works in detail in Year 11.

Examiner tip: Phytomining and bioleaching are slower than traditional mining, but they avoid digging up large amounts of rock, use less energy, and can extract metal from ore that wouldn't otherwise be worth mining. That's the trade-off examiners want you to weigh up.
